## 2. Summary in one paragraph

The paper closes seven cells of the `K_{3,3}` table, `z(12,18;3)=108, z(13,17;3)=110, z(13,18;3)=116, z(14,17;3)=118, z(14,18;3)=124, z(15,17;3)=126, z(15,18;3)=132`, by exhibiting a single *labeled* 13 × 18 matrix `M` with 116 ones (the "core") from which all seven lower-bound witnesses are obtained by deleting one row or one column and/or appending one or both of exactly two admissible weight-8 rows. **The upper bounds are not new computations**: six of the seven are the 2016 Collins et al. Table 4 entries (which Collins obtained by their `z_bound` recursion, i.e. star-counting + vertex-deletion, not by exhaustive search), and `z(13,18;3) ≤ 116` is re-derived from `z(13,17;3) ≤ 110` by a one-line column-deletion argument. The one genuinely new upper bound, `z(12,18;3) ≤ 108` (Collins had 109), is proved by a *uniqueness-plus-extension* argument: every 109-one 12 × 18 matrix must have column-degree multiset `{7, 6^17}`, deleting a degree-6 column lands exactly on the (12,17,103) extremal graph, which Collins marks unique up to isomorphism, and a brute-force check of all `C(12,6)=924` degree-6 column extensions of that graph rejects every one. No SAT solver, no Lean, and no proof assistant is involved anywhere; all finite claims are checked by a 100-line Python script in 1.5 s, and the single non-finite dependency (uniqueness of the (12,17,103) graph) is imported from Collins et al. and explicitly flagged as such.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 What a "core" is

A *core* here is nothing more than one fixed, labeled, `K_{3,3}`-free 0/1 matrix from which a family of witnesses is generated by three operations: delete a row, delete a column, append a row. It is a lower-bound (construction) device, not an upper-bound technique. The structure is:

```
M (13x18, 116)  --delete row 1-->      (12x18, 108)      [see 7.4: supplement ships a different 108-witness]
M               --delete col 1-->      (13x17, 110)
M + A (14x18, 124)           --delete col 1-->  (14x17, 118)
M + A + B (15x18, 132)       --delete col 1-->  (15x17, 126)
```

where `A = 100010010010111100` and `B = 000110110101010100` are the only two weight-8 rows among all `C(18,8) = 43,758` that can be appended to `M` without creating a `K_{3,3}`, and after appending either one the other is the unique remaining weight-8 extension. (Section 4: "This statement is deliberately local to the supplied labeled matrix M. We do not claim that M is the unique 13 × 18 extremal graph up to isomorphism, nor do we classify all extremal cores or all their possible extensions.")

### 4.2 How exactness is proved — cell by cell

*Lower bounds*: each witness is a plain-text matrix; `K_{3,3}`-freeness is checked by "both equivalent tests: every triple of rows shares at most two columns and every triple of columns shares at most two rows" (bitmask AND + popcount over all row triples and all column triples).

*Upper bounds*:

- **(13,17) ≤ 110, (14,17) ≤ 118, (14,18) ≤ 124, (15,17) ≤ 126, (15,18) ≤ 132**: cited from Collins et al. 2016 Table 4. Important for us: in Collins' Table 4 these entries are *undecorated*, and Collins' legend says "an undecorated number indicates that the bound was obtained by using Lemmas 2, 3 and 4, and without exhaustive enumeration". Collins even remarks "the interested reader may note other weak-looking bounds in Table 4, such as for z(k,17;3) for 13 ≤ k ≤ 17." Hou's witnesses show that these "weak-looking" recursion bounds are in fact tight at (13,17),(14,17),(15,17),(13,18),(14,18),(15,18). (Typographic decorations do not survive text extraction, so I am relying on Collins' legend + prose rather than seeing the italics myself.)

- **(13,18) ≤ 116** (Section 3, verbatim): "Collins et al. [2] give z(13,17;3) ≤ 110. If a K_{3,3}-free 13 × 18 matrix had 117 ones, some column would have degree at most ⌊117/18⌋ = 6. Deleting that column would leave a K_{3,3}-free 13 × 17 matrix with at least 111 ones, contradicting the upper bound. Thus z(13,18;3) = 116." (Collins' Table 4 already lists 116 at (13,18), so this is a re-derivation, not a new bound.)

- **(12,18) ≤ 108** — the only new upper bound. Section 5: "Table 4 of Collins et al. [2] records z(12,17;3) = 103 and marks the extremal (12,17,103) graph as unique up to isomorphism."

  **Lemma 1 (Hou).** *No K_{3,3}-free 12 × 18 binary matrix has 109 ones.*

  *Proof (verbatim).* "Assume that such a matrix exists. If a column had degree at most five, deleting it would leave at least 104 ones in a K_{3,3}-free 12 × 17 matrix, contradicting z(12,17;3) = 103. Every column therefore has degree at least six. Since 109 = 17 · 6 + 7, the column-degree multiset consists of seventeen sixes and one seven. Delete any degree-six column and call the remaining matrix M′. It has 103 ones and therefore lies in the unique extremal isomorphism class. Let R be the representative included in the supplement. There exist a row permutation π and a column permutation σ carrying M′ to R. If the deleted column has row-neighborhood S ⊆ [12], then under this isomorphism it is transported to the six-subset π(S). Reattaching the deleted column to M′ preserves K_{3,3}-freeness if and only if attaching the column with neighborhood π(S) to R does so, because row and column permutations preserve the forbidden-submatrix property. Consequently, testing all C(12,6) = 924 six-subsets on the single representative R covers every possible 109-edge counterexample. The verifier rejects every candidate extension, a contradiction. ∎"

  Section 6: "The only external mathematical dependency in the exceptional upper-bound argument is the published uniqueness of the (12,17,103) extremal graph in [2]; this dependency is stated explicitly rather than hidden inside the computation."

### 4.3 Verification architecture

`verify.py` (Python 3, stdlib: `itertools.combinations`, `hashlib`, `json`) does, in order: dimension/edge-count/triple checks for the seven witnesses; the 924 degree-6 column extensions of `z1217_103.txt` (all rejected); the 43,758 weight-8 row extensions of the core (exactly `A`, `B` accepted); the two second-extension searches (each yields exactly the other row); the chain identities (`core + [A] == z1418_124`, `core + [A,B] == z1518_132`, deleting column index 1 maps 116 → 110, 124 → 118, 132 → 126; deleting the unique degree-8 row of the core gives a K33-free 12 × 18 with 108 ones). It writes `verification_report.json`. The whole run is 1.5–1.8 s. Section 6 is careful that "The 43,758-row enumeration is not used as a global upper-bound proof for any Zarankiewicz number."

**No SAT solver, no ILP, no Lean/Coq/Isabelle, no nauty** appear anywhere in the paper or the supplement. Tan [5] and Davies–Gill–Horsley [6] are cited only as background.

## 5. Extracted lemmas, stated generally, with Lean-provability notes

The ZarPrune gate accepts a term of `Prune P` = a computable `kill : Profile m n → Bool` plus `sound : ∀ A, kill (profileOf A) = true → ¬ Valid P A`, where `Valid P A := ¬ HasKst P A ∧ P.w ≤ weight A`, `Mat m n := Fin m → Fin n → Bool`, sums are `sumFin`, and `HasKst` is an existential over strictly increasing index tuples `R : Fin s → Fin m`, `C : Fin t → Fin n`. Everything below is assessed against those definitions, Mathlib-free.

### L1. Column-deletion (density) prune — Hou §3, Collins Lemma 3

*Hypotheses.* `P = (m, n, s, t, w)` with `n ≥ 1`; a proved neighbour bound `hU : ∀ B : Mat m (n-1), ¬ HasKst (m,n-1,s,t) B → weight B ≤ U`.

*Statement.* For every `K_{s,t}`-free `A : Mat m n` and every column `j`, `weight A − colSum A j ≤ U`. Hence the profile prune `kill pf := ∃ j, sumFin m pf.row > U + pf.col j` (equivalently `min_j pf.col j < w − U` when the total is exactly `w`) is sound. Row dual with `z(m−1, n)`.

*Proof idea.* `deleteCol A j : Mat m (n-1)` re-indexes columns by the order-embedding `Fin (n-1) → Fin n` that skips `j`; the ones count drops by exactly `colSum A j`; any `K_{s,t}` in the deleted matrix maps to one in `A` because composing an `Incr` tuple with an order-embedding is `Incr`.

*Lean difficulty: moderate, no Mathlib needed.* Needs (i) the skip-embedding `Fin.succAbove`-style function (available in core Lean as `Fin.succAbove`, plus a strict-monotonicity lemma one can prove by `omega` on `.val`); (ii) `sumFin_succAbove`-type lemma: `sumFin n f = f j + sumFin (n-1) (f ∘ succAbove j)` — an induction on `n` in the spirit of `sumFin_swap`, maybe 40–80 lines; (iii) transport of `HasKst` back along the embedding, ~20 lines. The subtle design point is that the prune is *parametrised by another cell's proven bound*: it must be a function `(hU : UpperBound m (n-1) s t U) → Prune P`, so the harness has to thread proven cell bounds as Lean terms (or as trusted axioms with explicit provenance, which the README's "no escape hatch" policy would forbid). This is the same "neighbour-bound table" that Collins' `z_bound` DP and Tan's attack use, so it should be a first-class object in the pipeline.

### L2. Regularity forcing / profile pinning — the first half of Hou's Lemma 1

*Hypotheses.* As L1, plus the total `w`.

*Statement.* Under L1, every column has `c_j ≥ c := w − U`, and `Σ_j c_j = w`. So (a) if `n · (w − U) > w` there is no valid matrix at all (Collins Lemma 4, first clause: "If z(m−1,n;s) < z ≤ w − ⌊w/m⌋, then z(m,n;s) < w", transposed); (b) if `0 ≤ w − n·c ≤ n − 1` the column-degree multiset is `{(c+1)^{w−nc}, c^{n−(w−nc)}}` — in particular for `(12,18,109)`: `c = 6`, excess `1`, multiset `{7, 6^17}`; for `(13,18,117)`: `c = 7`, `18·7 = 126 > 117`, killed outright.

*Lean difficulty: easy given L1.* (a) is `sumFin` lower bound (`sumFin k f ≥ k·c` when all `f i ≥ c`, the mirror of `sumFin_le`) + `omega`. (b) is not needed as a *prune* — on `Profile`s the harness simply enumerates the surviving partitions and finds one — but as a *cover-completeness* fact it is a counting identity over `sumFin`, also `omega`-able once the min-degree bound is available.

### L3. Uniqueness-plus-extension closure — Hou Lemma 1, generalised

*Hypotheses.* `U = z(m, n−1; s, t)` known exactly; the set of extremal `m × (n−1)` `K_{s,t}`-free matrices with `U` ones is, up to row/column permutation, `{R_1, …, R_k}` (Hou uses `k = 1`); target `w` with `c := w − U` and `n·c ≤ w < n·(c+1)` (so some column has degree exactly `c`, cf. L2).

*Statement.* `z(m,n;s,t) < w` iff no `R_i` admits a column with exactly `c` ones that keeps it `K_{s,t}`-free; this is decided by `k · C(m, c)` `K_{s,t}` tests. (For `(12,18,109)`: `1 · C(12,6) = 924`.)

*What the source proves vs. what I infer.* Hou proves only the `k = 1, (m,n,w) = (12,18,109)` instance; the general form is my restatement and is immediate from the same argument. Note the argument needs the *exact* neighbouring value and its extremal classification — a much stronger input than L1's bound.

*Lean difficulty: not a profile prune, and hard.* The uniqueness input is a full classification of `(12,17,103)`-graphs, which Collins obtained by nauty-based exhaustive extension ("backwards paths") and which nobody has formalised. To use L3 inside ZarPrune one would have to either (i) axiomatise the classification (forbidden by the gate's design), or (ii) replace it by SAT: the residual case is small enough that plain SAT refutes it, see 7.3. So the right lesson for our pipeline is not "formalise L3" but "L1+L2 shrink (12,18,109) to one column profile; hand that to the solver". The 924-check itself would be trivially `decide`-able in Lean if `R` were given, but it proves nothing without the classification.

### L4. Collins Lemma 2 (Star-Counting) — the KST prune with the balanced lower bound

Verbatim (Collins et al. 2016): "Let G be a (m,n,e+)_s-graph with e(G) = m·d_L + r_L = n·d_R + r_R, where 0 ≤ r_L < m and 0 ≤ r_R < n. If (a_i) is the left degree sequence of G (and thus a_1 + ⋯ + a_m = e(G)), then
`(m − r_L)·C(d_L, s) + r_L·C(d_L + 1, s) ≤ Σ_{i=1}^{m} C(a_i, s) ≤ (s − 1)·C(n, s).`"
The right inequality is the KST double count (already flagged in `lean/README.md` as the next target); the left one is Collins' Proposition 1 (balanced compositions minimise `Σ C(a_i, s)`). Hou does not use Lemma 2 directly but every "published upper bound" he cites descends from Lemmas 2–4 via `z_bound`.

*Lean difficulty:* the right inequality needs a hand-rolled `t`-subset counting layer (as the README says); the left inequality is pure `Nat` convexity and is `omega`-hostile (binomials) but provable by an explicit exchange argument; for a *prune* only the right inequality matters, since the profile gives `Σ C(a_i, s)` exactly.

### L5. Local extension classification (Hou §4)

"Exactly two of the 43,758 weight-8 rows extend M." This is a statement about one labeled matrix; it yields lower bounds only. Not a prune. Trivially `decide`-able in principle (43,758 × 220 triple tests) but pointless to formalise.

### 7.3 Can plain SAT close the residual without the uniqueness dependency?
`scratchpad/lit/sat1218/run.py`, pysat 1.9 / CaDiCaL 1.9.5, direct 9-literal `K_{3,3}` clauses (179,520), column sums fixed to `(7, 6^17)` by sequential counters, lex-nonincreasing order on the 17 degree-6 columns (a sound symmetry break since column permutations fixing column 0 preserve the profile), 15-minute cap. Variant A: nothing on rows (1,702 vars, 182,684 clauses). Variant B: additionally every row sum ≥ 8 from `z(11,18;3) ≤ 101` (2,662 vars, 184,580 clauses).

**Result (2026-09-21, Apple Silicon laptop, one core per run, both runs concurrent): no answer from either variant after ≈16.5 minutes of solving; both processes killed by hand at 16:44 elapsed.** (The in-script 900 s interrupt never fired: pysat's `solve_limited` holds the GIL, so the `threading.Timer` thread was starved — a harness bug to remember, use `subprocess` + OS timeout instead.) So the single residual profile case that Hou disposes of with the (12,17,103)-uniqueness import is *not* trivially closable by a naive direct-clause CaDiCaL run, even with the column profile pinned and 17 columns lex-ordered. Reading: the uniqueness trick is doing real work, equivalent to a non-trivial SAT refutation. Follow-ups that should be run before drawing conclusions: (i) Tan-style split into the 96 fixed-(row,col)-profile instances, each much more constrained; (ii) the auxiliary-variable `AtMost2` encoding plus row-degree-monotone symmetry breaking from `encodings_zar.py`; (iii) cube-and-conquer on the first rows; (iv) a longer wall-clock budget with a certificate-producing solver (CaDiCaL `--lrat`). Logs: `scratchpad/lit/sat1218/run{A,B}.log`.

### 7.4 A small discrepancy in the supplement (not an error in the theorem)
The abstract says "Deleting one row or one column … produces the remaining witnesses", and `verify.py` checks that deleting the unique degree-8 row of the core yields a K33-free 12 × 18 with 108 ones — which it does, and that matrix is bi-regular (every row 9, every column 6). But the shipped `z1218_108.txt` is a *different* 108-witness (row sums 11,10,10,9,…,7, also all columns 6), and the verifier never compares the two. Both are valid, so Theorem 1 is unaffected; the "single core generates all seven" narrative is true for the row-deleted core but the file shipped for (12,18) is not that matrix.

## 8. Relevance to our design (implications)

1. **The strongest cheap prune is not KST, it is deletion against a tight neighbour bound.** Hou's whole upper-bound content is L1 + L2. In our pipeline this means the prune library must be parametrised by a *table of already-proven cell bounds* (Collins' `z_bound`, Tan's SAT results, our own closures), threaded into Lean as hypotheses. This is a structural change to `Prune P`: add a `Prune.ofNeighbour (hU : ∀ B : Mat m (n-1), ¬HasKst … B → weight B ≤ U) : Prune P` constructor, with row dual. It is also the natural way for a closed cell to "generalise to other cases" (proposal §2.3 last question): every new `z(m,n) ≤ U` immediately tightens the deletion prune at `(m+1,n)` and `(m,n+1)`.
2. **Which cells to attack.** Hou shows the Collins recursion is already tight on `(13..15) × (17,18)` — there is nothing to gain there. The action is where the recursion is loose: `(12,18)` was loose by 1 and needed a uniqueness argument; `(16,17) ≤ 133`, `(16,18) ≤ 140`, `(17,17) ≤ 141` (Collins explicitly wants 140), `(17,18) ≤ 148`, `(18,18) ≤ 156` remain recursion-only bounds. A first target for the SAT/prune pipeline is a cell where L1+L2 already pin the column profile to one or two partitions (as at (12,18,109)) and the residual is SAT-tractable, i.e. we replace Hou's external "uniqueness" dependency by a certificate. Section 7.3 measures exactly this.
3. **Residual cases are what SAT should see.** The 96 surviving row partitions × 1 column partition at (12,18,109) is a good benchmark shape for the harness: one SAT instance per surviving pair (Tan-style) or one instance with the column profile fixed and rows free (my variant A). The evolutionary search should be rewarded for shrinking this count, and the SAT solver should be run on the survivors to validate that the count is a faithful difficulty proxy.
4. **Reward signal.** Because extremal witnesses are near-regular (Section 6), a prune that kills near-regular profiles is worth far more than one that kills skewed ones; count survivors *weighted by closeness to regularity* (e.g. by `Σ C(a_i,3)` slack, or by the SAT time on a sampled survivor), not raw survivor counts.
5. **Certification bar.** Hou's package is a good model of what a *non*-formal but auditable closure looks like (plain-text matrices, stdlib verifier, JSON report, SHA-256 manifest, explicit list of external dependencies). Our Lean gate is strictly stronger for the pruning half; for the SAT half we still need LRAT/SR import (the `refuted` seam in `upper_bound_of_cover`). We should ship the same artefact kinds plus the Lean/LRAT certificates.
6. **Correction to prior work.** Hou §7 points out that [7]'s comparison table did not use Collins' tighter entries, so (14,17), (14,18), (15,18) were already exact after [7]. The thesis should acknowledge this when it cites the SuperUROP tables.

## 9. Difficulty signals extractable from this paper

- **Deletion slack** `δ_col := w − n·(w − U_{m,n−1})` and `δ_row := w − m·(w − U_{m−1,n})`: negative ⇒ the whole cell is closed by L2 with no SAT; `0` or `1` ⇒ the column (row) profile is pinned to a single partition; larger ⇒ the number of surviving partitions of `w` into `n` parts in `[w−U, m]` grows fast. This is computable in microseconds per cell and is what made (12,18) tractable for Hou and (13,18) free.
- **Number of surviving partition pairs after L1+L2+KST** (2 × 10¹² → 96 at (12,18,109)): the honest branch count the SAT phase will face.
- **Distance of a surviving profile from regular** (max − min row sum; KST slack `2·C(n,3) − Σ C(a_i,3)`): extremal witnesses are near-regular, so near-regular survivors are the hard ones.
- **Whether the residual lands on a known extremal class**: if deleting a min-degree column leaves exactly `z(m,n−1)` ones and that class is classified, L3 finishes in `k·C(m,c)` tests; if it leaves `z(m,n−1) − 1` or fewer, no such shortcut exists and SAT (or a `†`-marked second-best classification in Collins' tables) is needed.
- **Wall-clock of the residual SAT instance** (7.3) as the ground-truth calibration for the above proxies.

## 10. Open questions

1. Can the residual (12,18,109) case be refuted by SAT in the pipeline with an LRAT certificate, making Hou's Theorem 1 fully certificate-backed (no uniqueness import)? (7.3 is the first datapoint.)
2. How far does the deletion recursion stay tight along rows 13–15 for `n ≥ 19`, and where is the first cell where a uniqueness-plus-extension or SAT step is required? Hou's core has two weight-8 extensions; does the 15 × 18 witness extend to 16 × 18 (Collins bound 140)?
3. Is the bi-regular (9,6) 12 × 18 matrix (core minus row 1) a known design (it is a 2-(12,·) packing-like structure: 12 points, 18 blocks of size 6, every point in 9 blocks, every 3 points in ≤ 2 blocks)? If so, the core might be a known combinatorial object and other extremal cells might be built from designs.
