# Estimating SAT branch hardness without solving: SATzilla features, lookahead cutoffs, cube-and-conquer case studies, tree-size estimators, backdoors, proof size, LP slack

Literature notes for the upper-bounds thesis (Zarankiewicz z(m,n;s,t) via pruned case-split SAT + Lean-verified prunes).
Written 2026-09-21. Topic: how to estimate the *difficulty* of one partition-pair branch (row-sum vector, column-sum vector) of the Zarankiewicz CNF cheaply, so that the evolutionary reward can weight a verified prune by the solver work it saves.

Companion notes that this file cross-references rather than repeats: `alphamaplesat.md` (MCTS cubing, propagation-rate reward, the "hard tail" lesson, its Section 8.2 list of zero-solve proxies) and `chivilikhin2023_decomp.md` (d-hardness, Monte Carlo estimator with (ε,δ) guarantee, rho-backdoors, common-random-numbers fitness). The project's current implementation of the ideas below is `zar_ub/difficulty.py` (`Probe`: conflict-budgeted CaDiCaL run with escalating cap, root propagation fraction, log2 volume).

Conventions. Quotes are verbatim from the text-extracted PDFs (layout artifacts removed). "[source]" = stated in the paper; "[inference]" = my reading or transfer to our setting. Runtime numbers are the papers' own. `m x n` matrix, `s x t` forbidden all-ones minor, `w` target weight; a *branch* or *case* is a (row-sum vector r, column-sum vector c) pair; the CNF for a branch fixes those sums (Tan-style cube-and-conquer over partitions).

## 0. Executive summary

1. Nobody in the literature estimates the hardness of a sub-problem *without* running something. The best cheap signals are all *probes*: (a) the amount of unit propagation a decision triggers (march/`march_cu` decision and cutoff heuristics, AlphaMapleSAT's reward, Schur-5's binary-clause count), (b) statistics of a short CDCL run (SATzilla-2012 "clause learning" features, Haim-Walsh online linear model, Nejati's `propRate`/`conflictRate`), and (c) the time or conflict count of a budgeted solve, censored at the budget (every cube-and-conquer case study: cubes that time out are re-cubed). Static formula features (size, graph, balance, LP relaxation) add little beyond cheap probes for runtime prediction (Hutter et al. 2014, Table 3: "for all SAT benchmarks, using at most moderately expensive features yielded results statistically insignificantly different from the best", and cheap features were often enough).
2. Hardness in a case split is heavy-tailed. Keller: of 21,557 / 37,160 / 38,616 subformulas, 28 / 62 / 63 took > 900 s, the hardest ≈ 1.2-2.7 CPU h against an average of ≈ 7.5 s. Codish et al. R(4,3,3): per-degree-triple average UNSAT time ranges from 51 s to 1554 s (30x) and one triple with 3105 instances is 253 of the 358 total CPU hours. Schur 5 and Pythagorean triples both balanced their partitions by *measuring* (predictor runtime > 1 s, or number of binary clauses) and re-splitting. The reward should therefore be a weighted sum over killed branches with heavy-tail-aware weights, not a count.
3. Local pilot on 14 recorded column profiles of z(10,14;3,3) at weight 78 (Section 5): root unit propagation fixes *zero* cells in the fixed-sum encoding (so "UP lookahead after fixing sums", taken literally, is constant); one-decision-deeper propagation (mean implied cells per single-cell decision) is the best cheap ranker (Spearman rho = -0.65, p = 0.012, 0.05 s/branch); a 20k-conflict CaDiCaL run (0.35 s) gives rho ≈ -0.53/-0.56 through its propagation throughput; a 400k-conflict budget (≈ 10 s/branch, 3-40 % of the true work) gives rho = +0.61 via censored conflict count; static counting slack, max weight, log-volume and sub-case counts are all non-significant (rho 0.27-0.39). Sample is tiny; these are pilot numbers, not conclusions.
4. Two of the "proxies" are also prunes when they hit zero: the Gale-Ryser realizability test on (r, c) and the Roman/DGH linear program on the degree histogram. An LP infeasibility is a Farkas certificate, i.e. a nonnegative combination of already-proven counting inequalities, which is exactly the kind of `Prune P` that is easy to discharge in Mathlib-free Lean once the base inequalities are lemmas (Section 7, PL4).

## 2. What "hardness" means in these papers

* **Solver work on an UNSAT instance.** Chivilikhin et al. and Semenov et al. define hardness as total solver time over a decomposition, measured "in any appropriate units": seconds, conflicts or unit propagations (see `chivilikhin2023_decomp.md` Def. 3). They restrict to unsatisfiable formulas because on satisfiable ones "a solver may often get lucky". Our branches at w = z+1 are all UNSAT, so this is the right notion; at w = z the SAT branches are a separate battery.
* **Balanced sub-problems.** S11 states the two goals of a cutoff heuristic: "1) the runtimes to solve each of the subproblems are comparable and 2) the sum of these runtimes (at least) does not exceed the runtime of solving the original instance." Nejati (S10) formalises the target of a split as `T_total(phi, v) = max(t1, t2)` when both children are UNSAT (parallel view), and lists earlier metrics `pm1 = max{t(phi1), t(phi2)}`, `pm2 = t(phi1)+t(phi2)`, `pm3 = -(t(phi)-t(phi1))*(t(phi)-t(phi2))`.
* **Empirical hardness model (EHM).** S1/S2/S4: a regression model from instance features to log runtime of a *fixed* solver. S4: "Probing features ... are computed by briefly running an existing algorithm for the given problem on the given instance and extracting characteristics from that algorithm's trajectory-an idea closely related to that of landmarking in meta-learning."
* **Proof size.** The DRAT/LRAT certificate length is the *post hoc* hardness; S13 and S15 report it alongside runtime (Section 3.6).

## 3. Source-by-source extraction

### 3.1 Empirical hardness models and their features (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6)

**S1 (Nudelman et al. 2004), features that matter.** The paper introduces the 84-feature set later used by SATzilla. DPLL probing group, verbatim: "First, we run a DPLL procedure (without backtracking) to an exponentially-increasing sequence of depths, measuring the number of unit propagations done at each depths. We also run depth-first random probes by repeatedly instantiating random variables and performing unit propagation until a contradiction is found. The average depth at which a contradiction occurs is an unbiased estimate of the log size of the search space [11]." ([11] is Knuth 1975.) Result on kcnfs (random 3-SAT): "our best four-variable model achieves a root-mean-squared error of 19 seconds, while our full 368-feature model had an error of about 15.5 seconds"; "our most important feature (by far) is the linearized version of [the clauses-to-variables ratio], and our second most important feature is [another variant of it]... both of these remaining variables are constructed from local search probing features." The LP relaxation feature is defined there (the extracted glyphs are unreadable; S4 describes it, below). [inference] For our fixed-shape branches the c/v ratio is essentially constant across branches, so S1's headline feature carries no information *within* an instance; probing features are what remains.

**S2 (SATzilla 2008), feature list and cost.** Figure 2 of S2 lists the 48 raw features used in SATzilla07, including "DPLL Probing Features: 34-38. Number of unit propagations: computed at depths 1, 4, 16, 64 and 256. 39-40. Search space size estimate: mean depth to contradiction, estimate of the log of number of nodes." and "Local Search Probing Features: 41-44. Number of steps to the best local minimum in a run: mean, median, 10th and 90th percentiles for SAPS. 45. Average improvement to best in a run ... 46-47. Fraction of improvement due to first local minimum ... 48. Coefficient of variation of the number of unsatisfied clauses in each local minimum". Cost: "The computation time for each of the local search and DPLL probing features was limited to 1 CPU second, and the total feature computation time per instance was limited to 60 CPU seconds." "For SATzilla07, we excluded a number of computationally expensive features, such as LP-based and clause graph features." Typical cost: "feature computation took at least 3 CPU seconds: 1 second each for local search probing with SAPS and GSAT, and 1 further second for DPLL probing."

Example rows (trivial / prev / cheap / mod / exp RMSE; avg feature time prev/cheap/mod/exp in s): Minisat 2.0-INDU 0.94 / 0.58 / 0.57 / 0.55 / 0.52; 222 / 24 / 85 / 231. [inference] RMSE ≈ 0.5 in log10 means typical predictions are within a factor ≈ 3 of the truth; that is the accuracy ceiling of instance-level EHMs on heterogeneous benchmarks and sets expectations for any per-branch model we train.

**S5 (Hutter et al. LION 2013), which few inputs a forward-selected model keeps.** Table 2 (features and parameters selected jointly, with omission cost): for SPEAR-SWV the order was "Pre featuretime (35.9), nclausesOrig (100.0), sp-var-dec-heur (32.6), VCG CLAUSE entropy (34.5), sp-phase-dec-heur (27.6)"; for CPLEX-BIGMIX "cplex prob time (10.1) ... mip strategy subalgorithm (100.0)". ### 3.2 Online / partial-run prediction (S7, S8, S9, S10)

**S7 (Haim-Walsh 2008), Linear Model Predictor.** "Our method uses a linear model trained on data gathered at the start of search." "Using ridge linear regression, we fit our coefficient vector w to create a linear predictor f_w(x_i) = w^T x_i ... We predict the log of the number of conflicts as runtimes vary significantly." Features (Table 1; extracted names): backjump size, learnt clause size, conflict clause size, decision level statistics, and "log(WBE)" (the weighted backtrack estimator of S23, "The only feature that takes more than constant time to calculate"); statistics (min/max/mean/last) over an observation window that is opened only after the noisy start of a restart; "we exploit estimates from earlier restarts by augmenting the feature vector with all the search cost predictions from previous restarts." Accuracy is reported as "the percentage of predictions within a certain factor of the correct cost (the error factor)" (Figures 1-2; the curves are not extractable from the PDF text, so no numbers are recorded here). "Satisfiable problems are harder to predict".

**S8 (Haim-Walsh 2009).** Same features drive a classifier (logistic regression) for SAT/UNSAT and ridge models per restart strategy; "we found that predicting cpu-runtime is more effective as a selection criterion for restart strategies" than conflicts.

**S9 (Leventi-Peetz et al. 2021).** CryptoMiniSat internal counters "conflicts/second, blocked-restarts, restarts" from "already the first two solving iterations are enough to forecast termination of the attempt with good success scores" (binary classification of fast vs. slow termination on hard instances). Noted observation: "the occurrence of many conflicts at the beginning of the solving process" is associated with faster termination. [inference] This is the same sign as our pilot: high early throughput (propagations or conflicts per second) marks the *easier* branches.

**S10 (Nejati et al. 2020), learned splitting with probe features.** Labels are true runtimes of sub-formulas collected offline (expensive); the model ranks variables. Feature list "sorted based on their importance extracted from the trained models" (Table 1): `numAssigned` (#times v got a value through branching/propagation), `numFlip`, `numLearnt`, `numInTernary`, `numInBinary`, `LRBProduct`, `propRate` ("average #propagation over #decision"), `activity` (VSIDS), `numPropagations` ("number of unit propagations in the limited search"), `conflictRate` ("ratio of #conflict clauses over #decisions"), `totalReward`, `numBinary`, `numTernary`, `avgVarDegree`, `avgClauseDegree`. "We first removed the very heavy features like LP-based (linear programming) features". [inference] Every retained formula-level feature is either a clause-length count or a *limited-search* statistic; LP features were dropped for cost. `propRate` and `conflictRate` are exactly what a 20k-conflict CaDiCaL run gives us for free via `accum_stats()`.

### 3.3 Cube-and-conquer cutoff heuristics (S11, S13, S14, S18, S19; S12 not accessible; AlphaMapleSAT in sibling note)

**S11 (HVC 2011), the original cutoffs.** Two methods with a depth parameter D: "either the branches are cut off when exactly D decisions have been made (method A), or when the total number of assigned variables (decisions, unit propagations, failed literals, autarkies) is at least D (method B)." Empirically "Method (B) for determining the cutoff was vastly superior (diminishing the variability of the sub-instances enormously)". D "is determined ad-hoc such that sub-instances only take a few seconds (this seems to be around the optimum ...)". Section 5.2 then gives the refined rule: "for some hard combinatorial problems both objectives can be achieved by cutting off a branch if a certain fraction (say 10%) of the variables is assigned ... However, for the larger industrial instances, the number of decisions appears to be also of important to determine the hardness of a subproblem." Hence: "We combine both approaches by using the product of the number of decisions and the number of assigned variables, |phi_dec| * |phi_dec ∪ phi_imp|, as the cutoff metric." Procedure `CreateCubes*` (Fig. 3):
```
1  theta := 1.05 * theta
2  <F, phi_imp> := LAsimplify_and_learn(F, phi_dec, phi_imp)
3  if phi_dec ∪ phi_imp falsify a clause in F or |phi_dec| > 20 then theta := 0.7 * theta
4  if phi_dec ∪ phi_imp falsify a clause in F then return <A, C ∪ {¬phi_dec}>
5  if |phi_dec| * |phi_dec ∪ phi_imp| > theta * |vars(F)| then return <A ∪ {phi_dec}, C>
6  l_dec := LAdecide(F, phi_dec, phi_imp)
7  <A,C> := CreateCubes*(F, A, C, phi_dec ∪ {l_dec}, phi_imp)
8  return CreateCubes*(F, A, C, phi_dec ∪ {¬l_dec}, phi_imp)
```
with "theta := 1000 as initial value. Using a value which is a factor 10 larger or smaller hardly influences the resulting partition." The rationale for the 30 % decrement on a refuted node: "If the lookahead solver was able to show that the current node is conflicting, then probably a CDCL solver could have found the conflict faster ... By lowering theta, the mechanism tries to cut off neighboring branches before a conflict emerges." Decision heuristic: `eval_cls` (weighted count of reduced-not-satisfied clauses, "a clause of length k has a weight which is a factor five times larger compared to a clause of length k+1") versus the cheaper `eval_var` ("the number of assigned variables during the lookahead on x_i = 1").

**S13 (Pythagorean triples 2016).** Partition into exactly 10^6 cubes "ensures that the conquer time of solving most subproblems is less than two minutes, a runtime with the property that proof validating can be achieved in a time similar to the solving time." "A more effective partitioning heuristic picks the leaf nodes such that the number of assigned variables (including inferred variables) in those nodes are equal." "we observed that the best heuristics for Pythagorean Triples formulas however is to count the number of binary clauses in each node ... Selecting nodes in the decision tree that have about 3,000 binary clauses resulted in 10^6 subproblems." Cube sizes 12-49 literals; "The peak average of the cube runtime is around size 24, while the peak of the conquer runtime is around size 26." Second-level cutoff "based on the number of unassigned variables, 3450 variables to be precise." Averages over the 10^6 subproblems: cube 78.87 s, conquer 47.52 s, validation 60.62 s; validation "very similar" to conquer, growing "slightly faster". Table 1 (five subproblems; cube/conquer/sum times in s): the "#var" cutoff heuristic was consistently the worst of the four lookahead heuristics (e.g. 789.43 / 263.79 / 1053.22 vs Ptn-3-SAT 152.98 / 75.50 / 228.48 on the first row), and pure CDCL ranged 150-1373 s. Totals: 35,000 CPU h solve, 16,000 CPU h verify; DRAT proof "almost 200 terabytes".

**S14 (Schur Number Five 2018), an explicit "hardness predictor".** Cutoff by remaining variables with a self-adjusting threshold delta: "The mechanism stops splitting if the number of remaining variables in a node drops below the value of parameter delta. The number of remaining variables is a useful measure as it strictly decreases, whereas number of binary clauses can oscillate ... We initialize delta to 0 ... We only increase delta when look-ahead techniques can refute a node ... delta is set to the number of remaining variables in that node." Decrement per node: `delta := delta * (1 - f * d^e)` (d = depth, e = "down exponent", f = "down factor"); "Examples of effective values are e = 1.0 and f = 0.6, e = 0.5 and f = 0.1, and e = 0.3 and f = 0.02. For the final experiments we used e = 0.3 and f = 0.02." Hardness measure: "We observed that the number of binary clauses in a formula is a reasonable measure for the hardness of Schur number subproblems. The more binary clauses, the more constrained the subproblem (and thus easier to solve). For example, stopping with the splitting as soon as a formula in a node has more than 3700 binary clauses results in about 9 millions of mostly easy subproblems". Single-core optimum: "Let N be the number of cubes in a partition ... For low values of N, an increase of N leads to an increase of the total runtime-apparently some subproblems are about as hard as the original one. If we increase N further, the total runtime starts to decrease ... Yet when N becomes really large, the runtime increases again ... the optimal value for N is around 10 000" (Fig. 2, one subproblem). Predictor and balancing: "We used the mechanism to partition subproblems as a hardness predictor of subproblems by using a high down exponent and a small down factor: e = 1.0 and f = 0.1 ... It turned out that the runtime of partitioning with e = 1.0 and f = 0.1 is larger than a second for hard subproblems and significantly smaller for the easier ones. We extended the partition by splitting subproblems if this hardness predictor took over a second. Splitting was continued until none of the subproblems was predicted to be hard. To limit the size of the partition, we merged two cubes if they had the same parent node and the sum of their hardness predictor times was less than 0.1 second." Outcome: 10,330,615 top-level cubes, sizes 13-62 ("quite unbalanced"), yet binary-clause counts concentrated in 3650-3850; solve-time histogram: "A large fraction of the subproblems can be solved within 20 to 40 seconds. Most subproblems are solvable within two minutes and few are somewhat harder." 27,600 CPU h partition + 95,600 CPU h conquer; 65 billion cubes in total; "The computation of Schur Number Five and the Pythagorean triples problem differ in the balance between the partition phase and the conquer phase. For Schur Number Five, almost 78% of the computation was devoted to the conquer phase, while for the Pythagorean triples problem this was only 38%." Both chose "to continue splitting until the total runtime would start to increase (based on the solving time of randomly selected subproblems)".

**S18 (Ramsey R(3,8), R(3,9), 2025).** "there are two main factors to consider: one is the time it takes to generate all cubes, and the other is the quality of the cubes, which can be measured by the time required to solve the hardest cube." Stopping: "we used the number of eliminated variables as the stopping criteria ... eliminated variables ... either assigned a T/F value or propagated ... by a simplification solver (CaDiCaL with orderly generation)." Parameters: R(8,3) "Cubing was performed until 120 edge variables were eliminated. If a cube was not solved after producing a 7 GiB certificate, further cubing was applied, eliminating an additional 40 variables. A total of 41 cubes were generated"; R(9,3): 100 eliminated, +40 on failure, 2,486 cubes, 26 h wall clock, 289 GiB proofs. Table 3: R(8,3) cubing 1,360 s, simplification 1,217 s, solving 19,811 s, verification 22,328 s; R(9,3) 15,530 / 42,482 / 697,575 / 473,874 s. Also: sequential R(8,3) took 66,504 s versus 22,388 s total CPU for cube-and-conquer. [inference] Certificate size is used as the *hardness trigger* (7 GiB) - proof size as the difficulty measure, operationally.

**S19 (Kochen-Specker 2023).** "the generated cubes might exhibit imbalanced difficulty"; "AlphaMapleSAT iteratively generates cubes until the total number of cubes surpasses a predefined cutoff, which is based on available computational resources"; cubes that time out are "further divided into more cubes and solved accordingly." Table 4: sequential SAT+CAS order 20: 140.5 min, order 21: 1945.0 min; SAT-only: timeout.

**S12 (Handbook chapter).** Not accessible. From the search-engine snippet only: chapter 2, pp. 31-59, describes the two-phase method and the Pythagorean triples computation (2 days, 35,000 CPU h, 800 cores). Nothing beyond S11/S13/S14 is claimed here.

**AlphaMapleSAT** (sibling note): reward = propagation rate = BCP propagations / cube size; cutoff `n_elim`; the hard tail (> 5000 s cubes) dominates wall clock; re-cube on timeout with cutoff + 20 variables. **Cubing for Tuning** (S26): uses a small random subset of cubes as a representative sample to tune solver parameters online, and shows that "the conflict-reducing effect ... generalizes to the remaining unsolved sub-problems" - i.e. cubes of one instance are exchangeable enough that a sample predicts the rest [source, Fig. 1 description].

### 3.4 Tree-size estimators (S23, S24 via S23/S1, S25)

**Knuth's estimator** (S23, restating S24): "Knuth's method estimates N, the size of a backtrack tree as 1 + b1 + b1.b2 + ... where bi is the branching rate observed at depth i using random probing (Knuth 1975). For binary trees, if dk is the depth of a random probe, then bi = 2 for all i ≤ dk and 0 otherwise, so N is 2^{dk+1} - 1. Knuth's method simply averages this estimate over multiple random probes ... This is an unbiased estimator". Limitations: "it requires that the search tree be available in advance of the actual search" (learning and restarts make the CDCL tree opaque); variance "grows as Theta(2^n)" on tall skinny trees; "If p ≥ 1/16, we hit the heavy tail and variance in the estimated search tree size is infinite" (p = heuristic inaccuracy in their model).
**Weighted backtrack estimator** (S23): during chronological backtracking, `N ≈ sum_{d in D} prob(d) (2^{d+1} - 1) / sum_{d in D} prob(d)` with `prob(d) = 2^{-d}` over the multiset D of branch lengths seen so far; unbiased after the first branch. **Recursive estimator**: exact count of the explored left part plus F(right subtree) with the simplest `F = leftsize`; O(n) per estimate. Experiments: 200 UNSAT random 3-SAT instances with 300 vars (satz215, mean 17,755 nodes), pigeon-hole 8 (26,881 nodes) and BF circuit instances; the plots (Figs. 3-5, ratio estimate/actual vs percent of search) are not extractable, and the text does not state numeric errors.
**S25 (Lelis et al. 2013).** Two-step Stratified Sampling (TSS) extends Knuth/Chen: "Stratified Sampling by Chen [1992] ... reduces the variance" via a type system; "if given sufficient time and memory, the prediction produced by TSS converges to the actual EST size"; applied to DFBnB over graphical models. [inference] The natural "type" for our trees is the partition pair itself: sampling sub-cases stratified by (r, c) is Chen's scheme, which is what Semenov's estimator does with uniform sampling.

### 3.5 Backdoors (S21, S20, S22; Chivilikhin in sibling note)

**S21 (Williams-Gomes-Selman 2003).** Definitions: a (weak) backdoor S is a set of variables such that some assignment to S makes the rest solvable by the poly-time sub-solver; "a nonempty subset S of the variables is a strong backdoor in C for A if for all [assignments to S the residual is solved by A]". Empirical motivation: "logistics planning problem (logistics.d.cnf) contains a backdoor with only 12 variables out of a total of nearly 7,000 variables."
**S20 (Kilby et al. 2005), correlation with DPLL hardness (satz).** Abstract: "Problem hardness appears to be correlated with the size of strong backdoors, and weakly correlated with the size of the backbone, but does not appear to be correlated to the size of weak backdoors nor their number." Table 3 (Pearson r, coefficient of determination): uf20: smallest strong backdoor s_F r = 0.71 (0.50), s_F + guess 0.78 (0.61), smallest weak backdoor w_F 0.58 (printed c.o.d. 0.00, apparently a typesetting error), backbone size -0.88 (0.78); uuf50 (UNSAT): s_F 0.74 (0.54); uf50: s_F 0.37, w_F 0.26, backbone -0.46; uf100-uf225 and RTI: w_F r between -0.13 and 0.07 (c.o.d. ≤ 0.02); backbone size 0.16-0.48; flat30/flat50: number of weak backdoors -0.31 / -0.37, number of models 0.42 / 0.54. Strong backdoors were computed by exhaustive search "limited to problems with up to 50 variables". [inference] Strong-backdoor size is a meaningful hardness correlate but is exponentially expensive to compute; nothing here is usable as a *cheap* per-branch proxy. What survives is Semenov's reframing: a decomposition set is a *chosen* backdoor, and its quality is measured by sampling.
**S22 (Semenov et al. 2018), Monte Carlo estimate of decomposition hardness.** "we randomly generate N assignments beta^1, ..., beta^N of variables from B and track the runtime of A on C[beta^j / B] ... The sample mean xi computed according to (4) can be considered as an estimation of an average time required by A to solve an average subproblem C[beta/B]. By multiplying it by 2^{|B|}, an estimation of the total time to solve all C[beta/B], beta in {0,1}^{|B|}, is constructed." For time-limited runs they estimate the *probability* P_B(t) of finishing within t (a Bernoulli variable, so bounded variance) and define the resistance function `G(B) = 2^s * t * 3 / xi_bar` (eq. 5). Cost caveat: "computing the resistance function in one point is actually quite expensive: if we use a random sample of size N and time limit t, in the worst case it takes N x t". The (ε,δ) sample-size bound for the runtime version is in the sibling note (Chebyshev: N ≥ Var/(ε² δ E²)).

### 3.6 Proof size as difficulty (S13, S15, S18; proof complexity background)

* S13: validation time ≈ conquer time per cube (60.62 s vs 47.52 s average), "Validation runtimes grow slightly faster compared to conquer runtimes." So checked-proof length is a faithful, monotone surrogate of solver work on these instances [source + inference].
* S15 (Keller): Table 3, "No. Hard" = subformulas needing > 900 s; s=3: 21,557 subformulas, 43.27 CPU h total, 7.23 s average, 28 hard, hardest ≈ 1.2 h; s=4: 37,160 subformulas, 77.00 h, 7.46 s, 62 hard, ≈ 2.7 h; s=6: 38,616 subformulas, 81.85 h, 7.63 s, 63 hard, ≈ 1.25 h. Proofs: "The combined size of all unsatisfiability proofs of the subformulas of s = 6 is 224 gigabytes in the binary DRAT proof format. These proofs contained together 6.18 * 10^9 proof steps ... The DRAT-trim proof checker only used 6.39 * 10^8 proof steps to validate the unsatisfiability of all subformulas. In other words, almost 90% of the clauses generated by CaDiCaL are not required". Section 6.3: "one subformula was significantly harder to solve compared to the other subformulas" for each s; they added case-specific symmetry breaking and "replace the hard case for each s in {3,4,6} by the corresponding 33 cases". [inference] The two operational lessons: (i) the hardest case is found by *solving*, then split by hand; (ii) trimmed proof length is ~10x smaller than emitted length, so if proof size is used as a label, use the trimmed (LRAT) size.
* S18 uses certificate size (7 GiB) as the re-cube trigger (Section 3.3).
* Proof complexity [not read here]: resolution (hence CDCL) needs exponential-size refutations of pigeonhole-type cardinality contradictions (Haken 1985), while cutting planes refutes them polynomially (Cook-Coullard-Turán 1987). [inference] A partition-pair branch that is empty *for a counting reason* (KST/Roman slack ≤ 0, Argument D, Gale-Ryser) can be extremely expensive for CDCL yet trivial for a Lean-checked counting lemma; that is the whole value proposition of evolved counting prunes, and it means "hard for CaDiCaL" and "killable by a short proof" are not opposites. The reward should therefore prize kills of branches that are hard *for the solver*, which is what all the proxies below estimate.

### 3.7 LP relaxation slack (S4, S1, S28, S29)

* SATzilla's LP relaxation (S1/S4): each clause becomes a linear constraint over relaxed 0/1 variables; features are "Integer slack vector: mean, variation coefficient, min, and max" (distance of each LP variable from an integer), "Ratio of integer vars in LP solution", "Objective value of LP solution". Cost class "moderate"; dropped from SATzilla07 as "computationally expensive"; dropped by Nejati as "very heavy". No source ranks LP features as top predictors for SAT runtime.
* Domain LP (S28): "Roman's proof implicitly shows that these bounds arise as the optimal values of the linear program with variables n_{s-1}, ..., n_m that maximises sum_{i=s-1}^{m} n_i subject to the constraints sum_{i=s-1}^{m} n_i = n and sum_{i=s-1}^{m} n_i * C(i, s) ≤ (t-1) C(m, s)" (n_i = number of columns of weight i, Theorem 1.1: `z(m,n;s,t) ≤ ((t-1) C(m,s) / C(k,s-1) ... + (k+1)(s-1)/s * n)`-style bound (1)). DGH add "valid degree-count constraints to Roman's linear program" (S29's summary) and improve many small bounds.
* S29 (dfield) computed the DGH relaxation at (9,23), weight 104: exact fractional optimum 314/3 = 104 2/3 at n_4 = 31/3, n_5 = 38/3; "each of the three integer degree profiles that survives Roman's inequality at weight 104 also satisfies the Davies-Gill-Horsley degree constraints. Their relaxation therefore cannot see the last contradiction." `analysis/dgh_boundary.json` lists per-constraint slacks at the optimum (e.g. `roman_triple_capacity` lhs 168 = rhs 168, slack 0). For Z(10,23) they used exact SCIP with VIPR certificates on "column-type" 0/1 models (`models/column_types_10x23_exact_113.lp`) and record that "Lean does not replay DRAT/LRAT or VIPR".
* [inference] For a *branch* (r, c fixed), the Roman LP is already decided (both constraints are functions of c alone), so "LP slack" of the KST/Roman constraint is a static number per branch: `slack_col = (t-1) C(m,s) - sum_j C(c_j, s)` and `slack_row = (s-1) C(n,t) - sum_i C(r_i, t)`. It is a prune when negative and, in the pilot, a weak ranker when positive (rho = +0.31). A richer LP per branch (column-type variables as in dfield) would cost far more than a solver probe and, by S4's evidence, would not beat it as a ranker. Its real value is as a *certificate generator* (Section 7, PL4).

### 3.8 Degree-based case splits in the mathematics literature (S16, S17, S27)

* S16 (Codish et al.): Table 1, "Proving that any (4,3,3;30) Ramsey coloring is <13,8,8> regular": per degree triple of v1: (16,8,5): 54 instances, avg 51 s, 0.77 h; (16,7,6): 270, 420 s, 31.50 h; (15,8,6): 90, 93 s, 2.32 h; (15,7,7): 162, 1554 s, 69.94 h; (14,8,7): 3105 = 115*3*9 instances, 294 s, 253.40 h. Instances have ≈ 32-34k clauses and ≈ 5.3k variables. [inference] Total work = (number of sub-cases) x (average time), and *both* factors vary by an order of magnitude across the top-level degree cases; the count is known before solving, the average is not.
* S17 (Gauthier-Brown): "there must exist a vertex v in V of degree d in {8, 10, 12}" (parity + degree bounds), then "we estimated that this approach would take more than 16,000 CPU days ... we regroup graphs that are similar to each other ... in what we call generalizations ... reduces, with the help of a simplicity heuristic, the total computation time to less than 950 CPU days". Lemma 5 is a pure parity prune ("every vertex has odd degree" forces |V| even). Full proof in HOL4 with the splitting argument formalised.
* S27 (Tan): Algorithm 1 generates column partitions "in lexicographic order - partitions of w into n parts in [0, m]" pruning by Argument A (`sum_i C(p_i, a) ≤ (b-1) C(m, a)`) and Argument I (prefix minors vs known z values); "Once all possible row and column partitions have been obtained argument D can then be used to remove partition pairs". Conclusion: "The CNF instances we generated for each set of parameters did not split the problem into cases finer than specific combinations of row and column partitions. Combined with the use of just one processor at a time, this imposed a limit on how far our new results could reach with reasonable computational effort at around z = 100. Parallelisation and further splitting outside the SAT solver (e.g. enumerating all possible ways to assign the first two rows and columns, each way leading to its own sub-case) ... would help ... argument D could exclude certain partial assignments without any further solving required". Tan reports no per-branch timings.

**Observations.**
1. The literal "unit-propagation lookahead after fixing the sums" is uninformative here: Tan's/our exact sequential-counter encoding is UP-complete for cardinality (S27 Section 3.2: "If exactly k of the b_i are assigned true, unit propagation alone will assign the other b_i false") but fixing a column *sum* to w with 0 < w < m forces no individual cell. The first useful level is one decision deeper (march's `eval_var`, AMS's propagation rate), and its sign is negative: branches where a single cell forces more of the matrix are the easy ones. This matches S14 ("The more binary clauses, the more constrained the subproblem (and thus easier to solve)") and S9.
2. CaDiCaL does ≈ 55k conflicts/s on these 6.5k-variable / 15k-clause instances, so 20k conflicts is ≈ 0.1 % of a 9-363 s solve; even so, throughput in that window (propagations, wall time) ranks branches at rho ≈ -0.5. At 100k the signal vanishes; at 400k (≈ 3-40 % of the work) the censored conflict count becomes the best ranker (+0.61) but two of 14 branches are solved outright. 3. All static counting features are weak and positively signed (more slack / larger volume -> harder), consistent with the alphamaplesat note's earlier reading of the same data: static features prune, they do not rank.
4. This is a pilot to be repeated on the E3 training-ladder tables (many more branches, uncensored conflict counts as labels) before any proxy is hard-wired into the reward.

## 6. Ranked, implementable difficulty proxies for a partition-pair branch

Each branch (r, c) has a fixed CNF `F(r,c)`; all proxies below are functions of `F(r,c)` only, hence independent of any candidate prune, computable once and cached in the case table, and impossible for the LLM to game by rewriting its prune. Costs are for the z(10,14) size (6.5k vars); they scale roughly with clause count (≈ C(m,3)·n aux clauses).

**P1. Conflict-budgeted CaDiCaL run with escalating cap, censored (the current `Probe.difficulty`).** Definition: run `solve_limited` with `conf_budget = B_k = B_0 * 4^k` until refuted or `B_k = B_max`; difficulty = conflicts if refuted, else `B_max` (or the last budget) flagged "open". Cost: geometric, dominated by the last budget (≈ B_max / 55k s here). Evidence: the ground-truth measure in S22/Chivilikhin ("conflicts" as a workload unit) and the operational rule in every case study (S15, S18, S19, AMS: time out -> split further). Local: rho +0.61 at 400k; nothing at ≤ 100k. Use: as the *label* on the training ladder (cells small enough that every branch finishes), and as the top tier of the estimate on target cells with `B_max` chosen so that a sampled quantile (e.g. 70 %) of branches finish (S13/S14 chose cutoffs so "most subproblems" finish in ~2 min). Keep the DRAT proof of refuted branches: that is a `refuted` seam obligation for `upper_bound_of_cover`, so the probe is not wasted work.

**P2. Propagation-rate lookahead one decision deep (march `eval_var` / AMS propagation rate).** Definition: with the branch CNF loaded, for each cell literal l among the 2mn cell literals, `k(l) = |cells implied by propagate([l])|` (or "failed"); features: mean k(l), max k(l), number of failed literals, and after root UP the fraction of fixed cells (zero in our encoding, but nonzero once the evolved prunes or symmetry clauses add units). Cost: 2mn BCP calls, 0.05 s here, ~0.5 s at (13,23). Evidence: S11 (assigned-variable cutoff "vastly superior"; "10 % of variables assigned" balances hard combinatorial sub-instances), S13 (equalise assigned variables), S14 (binary clauses), AMS (reward), S10 (`propRate` among top features). Local: best cheap ranker, rho -0.65. Use: primary cheap difficulty score `h_2(r,c) = -mean k(l)` (larger propagation = easier), calibrated against P1 labels on the training ladder by isotonic or log-linear regression.

**P3. Short fixed-budget CDCL statistics (SATzilla-2012 clause-learning / Haim-Walsh / Nejati style).** Definition: one run with `conf_budget = 20k` (0.35 s); record propagations, decisions, restarts, wall time, and, if the solver exposes them, learned-clause length and LBD quantiles (PySAT's `accum_stats` gives the first four; learned-clause statistics need the CaDiCaL binary's verbose output or `--stats`). Features: propagations/conflict, decisions/conflict, seconds. Evidence: S4 features 91-108 ("cheap"), Table 3 (cheap set ≈ full set), S7 (linear model on early-restart statistics predicting log conflicts), S9 (first two iterations suffice for a fast/slow classification), S10 (`propRate`, `conflictRate`, `numLearnt` top-ranked), S5 (timing features selected first). Local: rho ≈ -0.5 to -0.56 at 20k; unstable at 100k. Use: secondary features in the same calibrated model as P2; also its own wall time is a feature (S5).

**P4. Number of surviving finer sub-cases (Tan's own difficulty driver).** Definition: for a branch (r, c), count the next-level cases the harness would emit if it split further: e.g. the Gale-Ryser- and Argument-D-compatible row profiles for a column profile (this file's `subcases_GR_D`), or the number of admissible supports of the heaviest row (Tan's suggested "enumerating all possible ways to assign the first two rows and columns"). Cost: enumeration, milliseconds to seconds; exact, no solver. Evidence: S16 Table 1 (3105 x 294 s vs 54 x 51 s: count is a first-order term of total work), S17 (16,000 -> 950 CPU days by regrouping cases), S23/S25 (Knuth/Chen: tree size = product of branching rates; a count at one depth is the first factor). Local: rho +0.28 (n.s.) for the row-partition count; it was constant (1644) before Argument D. Use: multiplicative "volume" factor for *total* remaining work estimates and for the sampling estimator (P6), not as a per-branch ranker; when the count is 0 it is a prune (PL1, PL3).

**P5. Counting/LP slack (KST-Roman, Argument D, DGH degree constraints).** Definition: `slack_col = (t-1) C(m,s) - sum_j C(c_j,s)`, `slack_row = (s-1) C(n,t) - sum_i C(r_i,t)`, Argument-D slack for the heaviest row/column, and the DGH degree-count constraint slacks; optionally the LP optimum of a column-type model per branch. Cost: O(m+n) integer arithmetic; an LP is "moderate" (S4) to "very heavy" (S10). Evidence: SATzilla keeps LP features but never ranks them highly; S29: the DGH relaxation is exact-optimum 104 2/3 at (9,23) and "cannot see the last contradiction". Local: rho +0.31 (n.s.). Use: prune when ≤ 0 (with a Farkas certificate, PL4); as a ranker only as a tie-breaker.

**P6. Sampling-based estimator over sub-cases (Semenov / Chivilikhin / Knuth).** Definition: for a set S of branches (e.g. the survivors of a candidate prune), draw N branches uniformly (or stratified by P2 score, Chen's type system), run P1 with budget t, and estimate total work as `|S| * mean(work)` or, robustly, `|S| * t * (1/P_hat(t)) * 3` (S22 eq. 5) where `P_hat(t)` is the fraction finishing within t. Cost: N x t. Evidence: S22, Chivilikhin Theorem 3 ((ε,δ) bound), S26 (a cube sample generalises to the rest of the same instance), S13/S14 ("based on the solving time of randomly selected subproblems"). Use: this is the estimate of *remaining work after a prune*, i.e. the denominator of the reward; use common random numbers (one fixed sample per generation) so candidate prunes are compared with low variance (see chivilikhin note Section 6.2).

**P7. Proof size of already-refuted branches (label, not proxy).** Definition: trimmed LRAT length (or DRAT-trim "proof steps") for branches solved on the ladder. Evidence: S13 (validation ≈ conquer), S15 (trimmed = 10 % of emitted), S18 (7 GiB trigger). Use: alternative label to conflicts when training the calibration for P2/P3; also the honest unit for "work saved" in the thesis write-up, because it is solver-independent and checkable.

**P8. Search-space size estimate by random probing (Knuth; SATzilla features 61-62).** Definition: repeatedly assign random cell literals with UP until contradiction; average depth d -> estimate 2^{d+1}-1 (or mean depth as a feature). Cost: ≤ 1 s. Evidence: S1 (unbiased for a DPLL tree), S23 (huge variance; opaque to CDCL learning). Not tested locally; expected to be dominated by P2, which uses the same BCP calls with lower variance. Listed for completeness.

**Not recommended.** Strong-backdoor size (S20: correlates but exponential to compute); weak backdoor size (uncorrelated, S20); static graph/balance features (constant across branches of one instance); full LP relaxation of the CNF (cost without evidence of gain, S4/S10).

**Suggested composite.** `hardness(r,c) = P1 if refuted within cap, else calibrated_model(P2, P3, P4, P5) clipped to ≥ cap`, with the calibration fitted on ladder cells where P1 finishes; the reward for a verified prune = `sum over killed branches of hardness` normalised by the P6 estimate of the table's total, and a tail statistic (kills among the top-decile hardness) reported alongside (AMS Fig. 2 lesson).

## 7. Pruning lemmas extracted (with hypotheses and Lean-provability notes)

The ZarPrune gate accepts `kill : Profile m n -> Bool` with `sound : ∀ A, kill (profileOf A) = true -> ¬ Valid P A`, where `Valid P A := ¬ HasKst P A ∧ P.w ≤ weight A`, `HasKst` quantifies over strictly increasing index tuples `R : Fin s -> Fin m`, `C : Fin t -> Fin n`, and sums are `sumFin` over `Fin`. "Mathlib-free" means no `Finset`, no `Nat.choose` lemmas beyond what is hand-rolled.

**PL1. Gale-Ryser realizability (profile-level, K_{s,t}-independent).**
Hypotheses: r = (profile).row, c = (profile).col, arbitrary (unsorted) vectors.
Statement: if there is a set S of k rows with `sum_{i in S} r_i > sum_j min(c_j, k)`, then no 0/1 matrix has row sums r and column sums c; in particular kill when `sum_i r_i ≠ sum_j c_j` (already `mismatch`) or, for the k heaviest rows, `sum_{i ≤ k} r_(i) > sum_j min(c_j, k)` for some k (Gale-Ryser dominance; the converse also holds but is not needed for a prune).
Proof: `sum_{i in S} rowSum A i = sum_j (number of ones of column j inside S) ≤ sum_j min(colSum A j, |S|)` by Fubini (`sumFin_swap`) and the pointwise bound `sum_{i in S} ind(A i j) ≤ min(colSum j, |S|)`.
Lean: **medium**. `kill` sorts the row vector and checks k = 1..m (all computable on `Fin m -> Nat` lists). `sound` needs sums over a subset S; representable as `sumFin m (fun i => if i ∈ S then ... else 0)` with S given as a `Fin m -> Bool` mask, plus two small lemmas (a masked sum ≤ the full sum; a masked sum of 0/1 entries ≤ the mask size). No subset-counting over t-subsets is needed, so it is strictly easier than KST. Value: kills only profiles that are empty for every matrix, which the harness's partition generator may already avoid emitting; worth proving because it certifies that layer.

**PL2. Kővári-Sós-Turán / Roman capacity (column and row forms).**
Hypotheses: `¬ HasKst P A`.
Statement: `sum_j C(colSum A j, s) ≤ (t-1) C(m, s)` and `sum_i C(rowSum A i, t) ≤ (s-1) C(n, t)`; kill when either fails on the profile.
Proof: double count pairs (s-subset of rows, column containing it); each s-subset is contained in ≤ t-1 columns (else a K_{s,t} with increasing index tuples exists), and column j contains C(c_j, s) of them.
Lean: **hard** in the Mathlib-free core (README already lists it as the next target): needs an enumeration of s-subsets of `Fin m` as increasing tuples, a bijection/count `C(k, s)` for the number of increasing tuples inside a set of size k, and the pigeonhole step producing `Incr R`, `Incr C` witnesses. Roughly: a hand-rolled `choose` with its recurrence, a lemma "number of increasing s-tuples in a k-element down-set is choose k s", and a counting-by-Fubini argument. Days of work, but reusable for PL3 and for most evolved counting prunes.

**PL3. Guy's Argument D (heaviest row / heaviest column).** As implemented in `zar_ub/cases.py::kill_row_argument_d`: with r_0 = max row sum and the r_0 lightest column sums c_{(1)}, ..., c_{(r_0)}: kill if `sum_{i=1}^{r_0} C(c_{(i)} - 1, s-1) > (t-1) C(m-1, s-1)` (and the dual). Hypotheses: `¬ HasKst`. Lean: **hard**, same machinery as PL2 restricted to (s-1)-subsets of the other rows; the "lightest columns minimise the LHS" step is a rearrangement inequality on sorted lists (elementary but fiddly over `Fin`).

**PL4. Farkas / cutting-planes combination prune (the LP-slack prune).**
Hypotheses: a finite list of *already proven* linear inequalities in the profile numbers, `L_k(r, c) ≤ R_k` (instances of PL1/PL2/PL3, `deficit`, `rowCap`/`colCap`, DGH degree-count constraints once proven), and nonnegative integer multipliers y_k.
Statement: if `sum_k y_k L_k(r,c) > sum_k y_k R_k` evaluates true on the profile, kill.
Proof: the weighted sum of true inequalities is true; contradiction with the evaluated numbers.
Lean: **easy** relative to the base lemmas: `kill` computes the two sides with the multipliers as data; `sound` is `omega`-level Nat arithmetic after instantiating each base lemma (core Lean 4 has `omega`; no Mathlib needed). This is the mechanism by which an *LP infeasibility* (VIPR-style certificate in S29) becomes a `Prune P` term, and it is the natural output format for an LLM-evolved prune: "these multipliers on these lemmas". The multipliers can be found by an exact rational LP solver outside Lean (as S29 does with SCIP/VIPR) and only checked inside.

**PL5. Deletion / Argument I with an explicit premise (Tan's Argument I; dfield's "deletion closures").**
Hypotheses: an upper bound `z(m-1, n; s, t) ≤ u` for a proper minor, supplied as a hypothesis (not proven inside the prune), and the profile.
Statement: kill if `w - r_min > u` (deleting the lightest row leaves an (m-1) x n K_{s,t}-free matrix with `w - r_min` ones) - and the column dual.
Lean: **easy conditionally** (a theorem `Prune P` parameterised by the minor bound as a hypothesis, exactly how S29's Lean exposes "historical premises"); the minor bound itself must come from a *verified* closure of the smaller cell, so the harness must track provenance. Row deletion must be formalised as a restriction `Mat (m-1) n` with `HasKst` monotone under restriction: moderate, but mostly bookkeeping over `Fin`.

**PL6. Parity-type lemma (Gauthier Lemma 5 analogue).** For bipartite matrices the only free parity fact is `sum_i r_i = sum_j c_j` (already `mismatch`); nothing new at profile level.

**Not prunes (explicitly).** (a) UP/failed-literal refutation of a branch: kills only via the `refuted` seam with a checked certificate (see alphamaplesat note, Section 8.4); (b) sorting/lex symmetry breaking: `notDescending_unsound`; (c) any hardness proxy: a proxy weights a kill, it never justifies one.

## 8. Design implications for the OpenEvolve reward

1. **Weight kills by estimated solver work, and never by count** (S14/S15/AMS: the tail dominates; E2 in `experiments/LOG.md` already shows heavy-tailed conflicts on tiny cells). Report both `sum hardness(killed)` and `kills in the top decile of hardness`.
2. **Compute proxies once per branch, cache in the case table, and keep them candidate-independent** so the LLM cannot inflate its score; the composite in Section 6 is the recommendation.
3. **Replace the root-UP proxy by the one-decision-deep propagation rate** (P2): the root UP fraction is identically 0 with fixed-sum sequential counters (Section 5, Observation 1). `difficulty.py::propagation_fraction` should be extended to `mean implied cells per cell decision` and `failed literals`.
4. **Escalate the conflict cap on target cells until a sampled quantile of survivors finishes** (S13/S14 practice), and stop trusting P1 as a ranker below that point (local: no signal at 100k conflicts, signal at 400k). On the training ladder, run to completion and use exact conflicts (and LRAT length) as labels; fit a small monotone calibration from (P2, P3, P4, P5) to log conflicts, in the spirit of S7's ridge model on early statistics, and validate it as S4 does (RMSE in log units, held-out cells).
5. **Use the sampling estimator with common random numbers for the denominator** ("fraction of remaining work removed"): one fixed sample of survivors per generation, evaluated once, reused for every candidate (S22, chivilikhin note Section 6.2, S26).
6. **Treat LP/counting slack as a certificate source, not a ranker**: an LLM-proposed inequality plus multipliers is a PL4 prune whose Lean proof is arithmetic; the search should be steered to produce such combinations, with an exact LP (rational simplex or SCIP-exact) as an oracle for finding multipliers before Lean checks them.
7. **Expect CDCL-hard-but-counting-trivial branches** (Section 3.6): those are the highest-value kills. A branch with tiny counting slack (P5 ≈ 0) but high P2/P3 hardness is the profile of a branch an evolved counting prune can plausibly kill; report this "gap" per branch to the LLM as context.
8. **Keep the DRAT/LRAT proof of every branch the probe refutes** so that probing on a target cell is amortised into the final certificate (`refuted` seam).

## 9. Open questions

1. Does the P2 signal (implied cells per decision) keep its -0.6 rank correlation on larger cells and across the E3 ladder, or is it an artefact of one instance size? (Needs ≥ 100 branches with uncensored labels.)
2. What cap fraction is needed for P1 to order survivors on a target cell such as (12,17) at w = 104 (where the flat instance took 13,625 s)? S13/S14 suggest choosing the cap by a sampled quantile; the sample size for a heavy-tailed distribution is the issue (Chivilikhin Theorem 3 with the Bernoulli form).
3. Does trimmed LRAT length rank branches identically to conflicts (S13 suggests yes on their instances)? If so it is the better label for the thesis, being solver-independent.
4. Can learned-clause LBD/length quantiles (S4 features 100-108) be obtained cheaply from PySAT's CaDiCaL binding, or do we need the standalone binary? Nejati ranks `numLearnt`-type features highly.
5. Which base inequalities should the PL4 machinery expose to the LLM (PL2 column/row, PL3, DGH degree-count families), and can the exact-LP oracle find multipliers that kill the branches P2/P3 call hard? S29's (9,23) result says the DGH family alone cannot; the "marked-row deficit" it needed is an overlap condition, i.e. a new base lemma, which is exactly what the evolutionary search should be asked to invent.
6. The Handbook chapter (S12) and Knuth (S24) could not be read; if the thesis needs their exact wording, obtain them through the library.
